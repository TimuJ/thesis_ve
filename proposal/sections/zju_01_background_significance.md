# Section 1: Background and Significance

## 1.1 The Rise of Long-Form Video Super-Resolution

Video super-resolution (VSR) has transitioned, over the last two years, from a per-frame restoration problem dominated by recurrent CNNs to a generative video synthesis problem dominated by diffusion models. **MGLD-VSR** (Yang et al., ECCV 2024) introduced masked guided latent diffusion that progressively refines per-frame super-resolved output while attending to neighbouring low-resolution frames, achieving substantially sharper detail than the recurrent-CNN baselines that preceded it. **Upscale-A-Video** (Zhou et al., CVPR 2024) introduced text-conditioned temporal diffusion at the U-Net level, producing smoother, perceptually cleaner output at the cost of some high-frequency detail. **DOVE** (2025) extended the evaluation framework with a re-curated long-form benchmark and a consistent diffusion-based degradation pipeline.

In parallel, the *application* demand for VSR has shifted from short clips toward long-form video: legacy film and archive restoration (typically tens of minutes per clip), video-conferencing recording enhancement (minutes-long calls), surveillance review (hour-long playbacks), and content-creation workflows that demand stable upscaling across an entire scene rather than just the first few seconds. The progress in per-frame diffusion VSR makes these applications technically conceivable for the first time. The problem is no longer "can we super-resolve a 1-second clip well" but "can we trust the output to remain consistent across the *minute* — without drifting in color, breaking at tile boundaries, flickering in brightness, or collapsing the identity of recurring faces."

Long videos magnify what short-clip benchmarks hide. Tile-based diffusion VSR produces seams every N frames (where N is the temporal tile size) that are invisible at 2-second clip granularity but extremely visible at minute scale. Diffusion sampling produces a slow per-channel color drift over thousands of frames that no per-pair metric can detect. Identity-preserving face SR produces drift in cross-clip face embeddings that only manifests when the same person reappears 30 seconds later. Periodic flicker from sampler artefacts is averaged away by clip-level perceptual aggregates. None of these failure modes show up cleanly on the standard SR benchmark suite.

## 1.2 The Evaluation Gap

Existing VSR evaluation can be partitioned into three families, each of which is structurally insufficient for long-video consistency assessment:

- **Per-frame fidelity metrics.** PSNR, SSIM, LPIPS, DISTS, CLIP-IQA are all defined on a single frame (or per-frame mean over the video). They are *temporal-blind by construction*: a video where every frame is individually high-quality but where frames N and N+60 belong to "different" colour distributions scores identically to a colour-stable video. No amount of clever per-frame metric design can recover the temporal signal.

- **Short-range temporal metrics.** **E\*warp** (TecoGAN; Chu et al. 2020), **tOF** (TecoGAN, optical-flow warping error), and **tLP** (LPIPS-of-warped-pairs) are defined on adjacent frame pairs (k = 1). They measure smoothness across frame boundaries but cannot see consistency across the longer time horizons where the most damaging long-video failures actually occur. Worse, adjacent-frame metrics suffer from a **smoother-output bias**: a spatially smoother output trivially has lower frame-to-frame difference regardless of whether it is more "consistent." This bias surfaces in our experimental work as the *tOF k-scale crossover* (Section 2.3): adjacent-frame tOF ranks the *worse* method higher; long-range tOF ranks correctly.

- **Perceptual / aesthetic benchmarks.** **DOVER** (Wu et al., ICCV 2023, *Disentangled Objective Video Quality Evaluator*) computes a no-reference quality score as the learned combination of two disentangled convolutional branches — an *aesthetic* branch trained on perception-driven labels and a *technical* branch trained on distortion-driven labels. **VBench-2.0** (Huang et al.) is a benchmark suite originally designed for **text-to-video generation evaluation**, scoring 18 dimensions across 5 categories of intrinsic faithfulness (physics correctness, common sense, human anatomy, motion order). While its long-video extension can be applied to SR outputs, it was not proposed for that purpose: 13 of its 18 dimensions require a text prompt against which to score, and the 5 remaining prompt-independent dimensions (Human_Anatomy, Human_Identity, Motion_Smoothness, Temporal_Flickering, Imaging_Quality) operate at clip level. Both DOVER and VBench-2.0 average per-clip scores up to a single video number; the long-range consistency signal we want is averaged away by construction.

A fourth, subtler problem: even when one of these metrics *should* catch a failure mode, its calibration is content-dependent in ways the metric itself does not surface. We document this empirically in Section 2 via a regime-shift case study: on close-up content, both Human_Anatomy and Human_Identity collectively rank the visually worse SR method as the visually better one. The metric does not flag "I am out of distribution on this video"; it simply returns a misleading score.

The cumulative picture is: there is no widely-used SR evaluation metric today that (i) sees temporal consistency at the time horizons where long-video failure modes actually live, (ii) is robust to content-dependent calibration shifts, and (iii) can be decomposed into interpretable failure-mode components that a researcher can use to debug a method.

## 1.3 Practical Significance for VSR Research

Without trustworthy long-video evaluation, the VSR field cannot do the things it most needs to do. *Honest method comparison on real long-form content* is impossible because the standard benchmark suite scores adjacent-frame smoothness, not long-range consistency, so a method that wins on the benchmark may lose on the actual use case. *Targeted ablations of architectural choices that affect minute-scale stability* (recurrent state size, autoregressive vs. tile-based decoding, classifier-free guidance strength, sampler order) cannot be guided by a metric that does not see what the ablation is supposed to affect. *Fair leaderboards for the long-video VSR sub-field* cannot exist because there is no measurement framework that takes long-range consistency as a first-class objective.

## 1.4 Scope of this Proposal

The contribution of this thesis is the **LR-VCC metric**, a parameterised **synthetic artefact test set** on which the metric is validated, and the **methodology** for designing reliability-weighted composite metrics that close characterised failure modes iteratively. The metric is designed as no-reference (so it can be applied to long videos for which no high-resolution ground truth exists), decomposable (each sub-metric can be inspected, replaced, or extended independently), and reproducible from a single CLI invocation backed by a JSON sub-metric cache.

Several extensions are *out of scope* for this proposal but documented in Section 7 (Timeline) as planned post-proposal work: a multi-person Identity sub-metric (designed in `docs/plans/2026-05-06-multiperson-identity-metric.md`); real-video baseline confirmation (sanity-check that LR-VCC does not false-positive drift on natural HR content); and a fast-varying brightness sub-metric to close the one remaining artefact family (flicker) that the current LR-VCC handles weakly at the composite level.

The remainder of the proposal is structured as follows: Section 2 demonstrates empirically that existing benchmarks fail (the killer empirical contribution); Section 3 states the aim and hypotheses; Section 4 enumerates research objectives and preliminary findings; Section 5 specifies the proposed LR-VCC metric and its validation; Section 6 lists expected outcomes and innovations; Section 7 details the timeline.
